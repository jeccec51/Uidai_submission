SECTION 1The question: “Have I seen this finger before?”

Picture a library with one card for every resident of India. A new visitor walks in and gives their fingerprints. Before we write them a new card, we must walk the entire library and check that none of the existing cards carries the same fingers. That walk is deduplication.

It is different from the everyday “unlock my phone” check. The phone already knows who you claim to be and compares against one stored card. Deduplication knows nothing — it compares against everyone.

SECTION 2Why a billion changes everything

Two forces turn a small, solved problem into a hard one. Think of them as the haystack gets bigger and the needles multiply.

Force 1 — tiny mistakes add up

Every matcher sometimes says “same finger” when it is really two different people. Say our matcher makes that slip once in every 1,000 comparisons. That is the “1 in 1000” false-match rate task (b) asks about. Now put the same matcher in two places.

How good must the matcher be?

Keep dividing 14.5 billion by the slip rate. Each step down the ladder makes the matcher 1,000 times more careful.

If each finger is checked only against the same finger of others (right thumb vs right thumbs), the count is 1.45 billion, and every alarm figure drops 10×. The lesson stays the same.

Getting a single finger that careful is very hard. Real systems get there by requiring several fingers to agree, plus iris and face. Two independent 1-in-a-million fingers both slipping on the same person is roughly 1 in a million million.

What this means for the review. The numbers papers report at “FAR = 0.1%” are a first sanity check, not proof of billion-scale readiness. For each paper we will ask: what happens far out in the tail, and how does it combine across fingers?

Force 2 — time adds up

First, be clear about what happens when one new person arrives. With the postcode way from Section 4 there are two very different steps, and only one of them repeats 14.5 billion times.

So the arithmetic is simply:

time for one new person ≈ time for one comparison (step 2) × 14.5 billion

Clock units · 1 second = 1,000 milliseconds (ms) = 1,000,000,000 nanoseconds (ns).

Make it physical. Let the fastest row (1.9 minutes) be 1 cm on a ruler. Then the AFR-Net-size postcode (57 minutes) is 30 cm, a school ruler. The second look (13.5 years) is 38 km, a drive to the next town. derived Looking at both prints is about 126,000 times slower than a 768-number postcode comparison.

More cores divide every row equally. With 100 cores, 57 minutes becomes about 34 seconds, which is workable. But 13.5 years only shrinks to about 7 weeks, for each new person. So the careful matchers can never run over the whole gallery.

The lesson of Section 2: use the fast postcode to throw away almost the whole gallery in minutes. Then let the slow, careful judge look only at the few hundred candidates left. At 29 ms each, 1,000 candidates take about 29 seconds. Keep this two-stage funnel in mind — every paper slots into one part of it.

SECTION 3What a fingerprint actually is

A fingerprint is a landscape of ridges, like ploughed furrows in a field. The overall flow (loops, whorls, arches) is shared by millions of people. What makes it yours are the small places where a furrow stops (ridge ending) or splits in two (bifurcation). These are called minutiae; a full print carries several dozen.

Full print

Rolled or flat print of the whole finger pad. Many minutiae, plenty of overlap between two captures. This is what most of the Aadhaar enrolment gallery looks like.

Partial print

Only a patch: small sensors, low pressure, dry or worn fingers, off-centre placement. Few minutiae survive, and the overlap with the stored print may be small. Task (b) focuses here because it is where matchers break.

SECTION 4Two ways to compare fingerprints

There are two families of matcher, and the difference between them decides whether something can scale to a billion.

The map picture of embeddings

Imagine every print placed as a dot on a huge map. A good embedding network places all impressions of the same finger in one tight neighbourhood and pushes different fingers far apart. A search is then “who lives near this new dot?” — and maps can be indexed (grids, trees, graphs) so you do not have to visit every house.

SECTION 5 · PAPER 1AFR-Net — a fast postcode, with a second look when unsure

Grosz & Jain, “AFR-Net: Attention-Driven Fingerprint Recognition Network”, IEEE T-BIOM 2024 · arXiv 2211.13897

The idea in one image: two experts look at the same print. One (a CNN) is good at local texture — ridge spacing, small shapes. The other (a transformer with attention) is good at how distant parts of the print relate. Each writes 384 numbers; together they form the print’s postcode. When the first comparison score is neither clearly “yes” nor clearly “no”, a second look lines the two prints up and scores them again.

SECTION 6 · PAPER 2JIPNet — a detective that solves “who” and “where” together

Guan, Pan, Feng, Zhou, “Joint Identity Verification and Pose Alignment for Partial Fingerprints”, IEEE TIFS 2025 · arXiv 2405.03959 · code + weights: github.com/XiongjunGuan/JIPNet (MIT)

The idea in one image: two jigsaw pieces. To decide if they come from the same puzzle you try to fit them — and once they fit, you also know exactly where one sits relative to the other. JIPNet does both at once: one answer is “same finger? (probability)”, the other is “how much is piece B shifted and turned?”. The authors’ point is that each answer helps the other.

Two things we must watch.
① Pose error is only shown as a figure in the paper (error against how much the patch was turned or shifted). No single number in degrees or pixels is given — so measuring it is genuinely new information from our task (b).
② Training data overlap. JIPNet’s training mix includes FVC2004 DB1_A/DB2_A and NIST SD14. Testing the released weights on those sets would be like grading a student on the homework they practised. Clean tests are FVC2002 DB1_A and DB3_A (the authors also treat these as unseen), and other FVC sets not in their training list.

SECTION 7 · PAPER 3HERS — searching inside a locked box

Engelsma, Jain, Boddeti, “HERS: Homomorphically Encrypted Representation Search”, IEEE T-BIOM 2022 · arXiv 2003.12197

The idea in one image: the gallery is a warehouse of locked boxes, each holding a fingerprint postcode. The warehouse keeper (the server) never has the key. Homomorphic encryption is a special kind of lock that still lets the keeper do arithmetic through the box walls: multiply and add the numbers inside without seeing them. So the keeper can compute similarity scores, still locked, and hand them back. Only the key-holder unlocks the scores and picks the best match.

The price: arithmetic through the box walls is slow and the boxes are bulky. HERS’ trick is to make the postcode shorter first (192 numbers → 64, 32 or 16), because time and storage grow with the length of the postcode.

Scaling to Aadhaar, honestly. derived If cost grew in a straight line with gallery size, 1.45 billion people would be 14.5× the 100 M test: ≈ 2 hours per query at 500 s/100 M on 10 cores, and ≈ 2 TB of locked gallery even at 32 numbers per template (140 GB × 14.5). That straight-line guess is ours, not the paper’s, and the paper uses synthetic fingerprints for its 100 M gallery. Encryption buys privacy, not speed — the question for UIDAI is whether privacy-on-the-server is worth that bill, or whether only part of the pipeline needs it.

SECTION 9The measuring sticks, from scratch

Task (b) asks for three numbers. Here is what each one means in plain words.

Every matcher produces a score; we pick a cut-off

Run a matcher on many pairs we know are the same finger, and many pairs we know are different fingers. Each pair gets a score. Plot how often each score appears and you get two hills. We draw a cut-off line: above it we say “match”. Drag the line and watch the two kinds of mistake trade against each other.

Impostors let in
false-match rate (FAR): red area right of the line
Genuine pairs rejected
false-reject rate: green area left of the line
Genuine pairs accepted
true-accept rate (TAR) = 100% − false-reject

illustrationTwo made-up bell curves to show the mechanics — not results from any matcher.

EER — the balance point

Slide the line until the two mistakes are equal. That shared error rate is the Equal Error Rate. One number, lower is better. Good for comparing matchers, but no real system runs at that point.

TAR @ FAR = 0.1%

Fix the line so that only 1 in 1000 impostor pairs gets in. Now ask: what share of genuine pairs still get through? Higher is better. This is closer to how a system is operated — and Section 2 shows why UIDAI would need to look even further into the tail.

Pose error

For a genuine pair, we know the true shift and turn (because we cut the patches ourselves). JIPNet guesses them. Pose error = how far off the guess is: degrees for the turn, pixels (and millimetres at 500 dpi) for the shift.

SECTION 10Our two tasks, restated

TASK (a) · Desk review — billion-scale applicability

A short report on each paper answering three questions:

  • Design choices — what kind of matcher is it (postcode or pairwise judge), what does it need as input, what does it assume?
  • Latency — how much time and storage per print and per comparison, and what that becomes at 1.45 B people × 10 fingers (derived, with the assumptions written down).
  • Failure cases — where it breaks (small overlap, poor quality, distortion, cross-sensor), and what a missed or false duplicate costs at this scale.

Output: where each paper fits in the funnel, and what is still missing.

TASK (b) · Hands-on experiment — partial prints
  • Run JIPNet (official code + released weights) and an AFR-Net-style baseline on partial patches cut from public FVC2002/2004 prints.
  • Report for both: EER and TAR @ FAR 0.1%, with the score hills and the trade-off curve plotted.
  • Report for JIPNet: pose error (degrees, pixels), and how it grows as overlap shrinks.
  • Failure analysis: show the worst pairs as images, group them by cause.

The data: FVC2002 and FVC2004

The Fingerprint Verification Competitions released small, well-known datasets. Each edition has four databases (DB1–DB4) captured on different sensors, one of them synthetic. Each “A” set holds 100 fingers × 8 impressions = 800 images; FVC2004 was deliberately harder (people were asked to shift, rotate and press differently, and to use dry or wet fingers). The prints are not partial by themselves — like the JIPNet authors, we will cut partial patches from them, with known shift and turn.

Known risks, written down before we start
  • FVC downloads come from the University of Bologna BioLab site and may require registration.
  • JIPNet’s README says the released weights suit only the paper’s test scenarios; fine-tuning may be needed.
  • JIPNet was trained on FVC2004 DB1_A/DB2_A — those must be kept out of any clean JIPNet score.
  • AFR-Net has no public training set of its size; our baseline will be weaker, and we will say so next to every number.
  • FVC is a few thousand images. It can show how each method fails on partials; it cannot prove billion-scale accuracy. Task (a) bridges that gap by reasoning, clearly labelled.
